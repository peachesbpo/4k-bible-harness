## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

## 📊 Diff Analysis: What Got Lost?

The ESV accurately captures the meaning of this verse. No significant translation loss detected.

**Explanation**: The ESV faithfully conveys the Greek:
- "Blessed" renders *μακάριοι* accurately
- "the merciful" renders *οἱ ἐλεήμονες* — from *ἐλεήμον* (mercy, compassion) — the ESV "merciful" is accurate
- "for they shall obtain mercy" renders *ὅti αὐtoὶ ἐλεηθήσονται* — the ESV captures the core promise: the merciful shall receive mercy

A subtle nuance: the Greek *ἐλεηθήσονται* is passive/middle voice, meaning "shall be mercy'd upon" or "shall receive mercy" — mercy comes TO them. The ESV "obtain" can suggest active acquisition, but in practice "obtain mercy" in English adequately conveys the sense of receiving mercy. This passive/active distinction is an etymological observation rather than a true translation loss.

The beautiful Greek wordplay — *ἐλεήμονες* (merciful) and *ἐλεηθήσονται* (shall obtain mercy) share the root *ἐλεη-* (mercy) — is preserved in the ESV through the shared root "merc-" in "merciful" and "mercy."

## 📝 Etymological Notes (commentary, not translation loss)

- **ἐλεήμονes** (*elemones*): From *ἐle-* (in, within) + *ηmo* (mover, one who acts) + *-nes* (participial suffix). Literally "those who carry mercy within" → "the merciful ones." The word conveys mercy as an internal quality — mercy is not just an action but a disposition, something held within.
- **ἐleηθσontaI** (*eleothsondai*): From *ἐle-* (in, within) + *ηθσon* (verbal suffix, to act upon) + *-dai* (passive/future ending). The word means "shall be mercy'd upon" — the passive voice conveys that mercy comes TO them, not that they actively acquire it. The future sense (*-dai*) promises this as something that shall be.
- **Shared root *ἐleη-*** (mercy): The root *ἐleη* appears in both *ἐleηmones* (merciful) and *ἐleηθσontaI* (shall obtain mercy) — the same concept of mercy/compassion connects the condition and the promise. Giving mercy and receiving mercy are etymologically the same act in Greek.
- The ESV preserves this wordplay through the shared root "merc-" in "merciful" and "mercy" — a rare case where the English translation captures the Greek etymological connection.

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

- **Mercy is its own reward**: The verse promises mercy to the merciful — those who have mercy shall receive mercy. The theological claim is that mercy is reciprocal: to give mercy is to be worthy of receiving it. The ESV captures this reciprocity through the shared root "merc-."
- **Mercy as internal disposition**: The Greek *ἐleηmones* reveals that "merciful" means carrying mercy *within* (from *� ἐle-* = in, within) — mercy is not just an action but an internal quality, a disposition of the heart. The merciful are those who hold compassion as part of who they are.
- **Receiving rather than acquiring**: The passive voice of *� ἐleηθσontaI* (shall be mercy'd upon) suggests that mercy comes TO the merciful — they do not actively "obtain" or "acquire" it. Mercy is given, not taken. This theological nuance — that mercy is bestowed, not won — is subtly present in the Greek.
- **The symmetry of mercy**: The verse creates a perfect symmetry: the merciful shall receive mercy. This is the theological heart of the Beatitude — those who embody a virtue shall receive that same virtue in return. The ESV preserves this symmetry: "the merciful... shall obtain mercy."
