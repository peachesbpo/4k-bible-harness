## 📖 Common English Translation (ESV)

> Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth.

---

## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

Blessed are the meek (literally "the bridled ones" — πraύς, from πra- + aύς, meaning *strength under control*, like a horse that has been tamed and bridled: not weakness, but power governed): for they shall inherit the earth.

## 📝 Etymological Notes (commentary, not translation loss)

- **πraύς (g4239)**: From πra- (forth/forward) + aύς (strength, force, might). The literal meaning is **strength under control** — the same word used for a horse that had been tamed and bridled. This is NOT softness or weakness; it is *governed power*. The ESV's "meek" has drifted toward connotations of timidity, which inverts the Greek sense. The classical valence is positive: someone who possesses strength and keeps it reined in, not someone who lacks strength entirely.
- **κληpo vouησouσiv (g2816)**: "Shall inherit" — from κλep- (to receive, take) + vouη (heirloom, inheritance). The word carries legal/formal connotations of inheritance — the earth is a legacy bequeathed to the meek, not merely something they receive passively.
- **γῆv (g1093)**: "Earth" — a simple noun meaning earth, land, ground. In this context, it means the earth as the totality of the world — that which the bridled shall inherit.

---

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

- **Strength under control, not weakness**: The Greek πraύς (g4239) reveals that Jesus is blessing those who *have* power but keep it *governed* — the bridled, the tamed. This is the opposite of praising weakness. The theological claim is that controlled strength — power exercised with restraint — is worthy of inheriting the earth. The ESV's "meek" risks reading as "the weak shall inherit the earth," when Jesus actually says "the bridled shall inherit it."

- **Inheritance as bequeathal**: The use of κλepovouησou (to inherit) suggests that the earth is a legacy — something bequeathed to the bridled by those who came before. The earth is not won by force but inherited by the governed. This frames the meek as heirs, not conquerors.

- **The earth as inheritance**: The promise that the bridled shall inherit γῆv (the earth) is radical — it suggests that the world belongs not to the powerful but to the governed. Those who restrain their own strength inherit the earth.

- **Parallel with 5:3**: Verse 5:3 says the poor in spirit possess the kingdom of heaven; 5:5 says the bridled inherit the earth. Together, they claim that both heavenly and earthly dominion belong to the humble and the governed — those who restrain themselves, not those who assert themselves.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- **πraύς (g4239)** is an adjective in the nominative plural, functioning substantivally: "the bridled ones." The word carries the sense of strength held in check — a horse tamed and bridled — not softness or weakness.
- **κλepovouησouσiv (g2816)** is a future verbal form — "they shall inherit." The future sense conveys a promise of what shall be, not what currently is.
- **τὴv γῆv** is in the accusative case — "the earth" as the direct object of "inherit."
- The verse follows the standard Beatitude structure: [Blessed] + [those with a quality] + [for they shall receive a blessing].

**Cross-References**:
- **Μaκariot** (g3107) — the signature Beatitude word, appearing in all 10 verses.
- **ὅti** (g3754) — the causal conjunction, appearing in every Beatitude.
- **αὐtoὶ** (g846) — "they," the subject of the promised blessing, as in 5:4, 5:6, 5:7.
- **γῆv (g1093, earth)** is thematically significant — it appears nowhere else in the Beatitudes, making this verse's promise uniquely about earthly (not just heavenly) inheritance.
- The concept of *governed strength* connects to Jesus's broader theology of self-mastery — those who can govern their own power are those who can be trusted with the earth.
