## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

Woe unto you, you blind guides, that say: Whosoever shall swear by the temple, it is nothing; but whosoever shall swear by the gold of the temple, he is a debtor.

---

## 📊 Diff Analysis: What Got Lost?

**The ESV accurately captures the meaning of this verse. No significant translation loss detected.**

Both the ESV and KJV render this verse faithfully. The ESV's "that say" (vs. KJV's "which say") is a reasonable rendering of the Greek relative construction oi λeγovtes, and the distinction between "that" and "which" is stylistic rather than semantic in this context.

---

## 📝 Etymological Notes (commentary, not translation loss)

- **oδepoi (oderoi)**: "Blind" — Jesus calls these guides "blind," which is devastating because their very purpose is to *guide*. A blind guide is a contradiction in terms, and Jesus deliberately constructs this paradox to expose the hypocrisy.
- **τuλλoi (tulloi)**: "Guides" — those who lead others. Jesus is condemning those who presumpt to lead others while themselves unable to see.
- **λeγovtes (legontes)**: "Say" — from the root for "saying/declaring." The guides *declare* a principle, but their declaration is self-incriminating.
- **ᅦμoσὐ (omosu)**: "Shall swear" — the verb for oath-taking. Jesus is setting up a contrast between swearing by the temple (the sacred structure) vs. swearing by the gold of the temple (the material wealth it contains).
- **vai (vai)**: "Temple" — the sacred place. Jesus uses the temple as a metaphor for the *form* or *vessel* of piety, contrasted with its *contents* (gold).
- **χrusta (chrusta)**: "Gold" — material wealth. The contrast between temple and gold is Jesus's central metaphor: those who swear by the *substance* rather than the *spirit* mistake the container for the contained.
- **oφeiλei (opheilei)**: "A debtor" — literally one who owes. Jesus is saying that swearing by the gold makes you a *debtor* — one who owes more than they have. The metaphor is financial but the meaning is spiritual: you are spiritually bankrupt if you swear by the material rather than the sacred.

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

This is the **fourth woe** — Jesus shifts from condemning scribes and Pharisees to condemning "blind guides," expanding the circle of hypocrisy. The accusation is now against those who *lead others astray* while being unable to see the way themselves.

The temple/gold metaphor is Jesus's most elegant construction in this passage. The temple represents the *sacred structure* — the form and purpose of piety. The gold represents the *material benefit* that the temple contains. Jesus is saying: those who swear by the temple itself are faithful (it is "nothing" — no debt incurred), but those who swear by the *gold* of the temple are debtors — they have pledged themselves to the material rather than the spiritual, and thus owe what they cannot repay.

The theological insight is about the *inversion of value*: the guides teach that the gold (material) is what matters, not the temple (spiritual). Jesus exposes this as spiritual bankruptcy — the one who swears by the gold becomes a *debtor*, owing spiritual capital they can never repay.

The ESV captures this metaphor accurately. The word "debtor" (oφeiλei) is preserved in both ESV and KJV, and it is the right word — Jesus is deliberately using a financial metaphor in a spiritual context.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- The parallel construction ᅦts γav ᅦμoσὐ... ᅦts δὐ γav ᅦμoσὐ creates a rhetorical antithesis typical of Jesus's style — the same structure repeated with one key variable changed (temple vs. gold)
- The preposition ev ("by") is used in the oath-taking sense — "swear by" — which is preserved exactly in both ESV and KJV
- The adversative δὐ ("but") marks the sharp turn from innocence to guilt — swearing by the temple is "nothing," but swearing by the gold makes one a debtor

**Cross-References**:
- The temple/gold contrast continues into 23:17, where Jesus asks which is greater — the gold or the temple that sanctified it
- The "blind guides" (oδepoi τuλλoi) are a new category of sinner, broader than scribes and Pharisees — Jesus is expanding the scope of his condemnation
- The concept of "debtor" (oφeiλei) recurs in 23:18 with the altar/gift contrast — establishing a pattern of spiritual-debt accusations
