## 📖 Common English Translation (ESV)

> You serpents, you brood of vipers, how are you to escape being sentenced to hell?

## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

*Original language nuances restored in readable English. Parenthetical notes explain the nuance recovered.*

O serpents (the Greek ὄφeιs is in the vocative case — a direct address, not a label; the Pharisees are being *called* serpents, summoned by that name), O offspring (the Greek gennhmaTa is also vocative — "offspring," the brood and issue of something prior, not merely "brood" but that which is begotten of something else) of vipers (the Greek ἐxiδnωv is genitive — "of vipers," the parent stock from which the offspring springs), how shall you escape (the Greek φύgheTe is in the aorist subjunctive — not "how are you to escape" but "how shall you escape," a question that presupposes the impossibility of escape) from the sentence (the Greek krίseo_s means "judgment" or "decree" — a formal verdict, not merely a "sentence" in the legal sense) of hell?

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

1. **Naming as Condemnation**: The vocative case of ὄφeιs ("O serpents!") and gennhmaTa ("O offspring!") is a rhetorical act of naming — Jesus does not accuse the Pharisees of being serpents but *calls* them serpents, summons them into that identity. The naming is itself the condemnation: to be named a serpent is to be condemned, for the serpent is the creature of deceit, of twisting and turning, of the serpent's nature that cannot be other than it is.

2. **Hereditary Vipers' Offspring**: The word gennhmaTa ("offspring/issue") continues the hereditary theme from verse 31. The Pharisees are not merely vipers but the *offspring* of vipers — the next generation of a violent lineage. The genitive ἐxiδnωv ("of vipers") names the parent stock: the Pharisees are begotten of vipers, and their nature is inherited, not chosen. This connects to Jesus's consistent theme of inherited identity.

3. **The Rhetorical Impossibility of Escape**: The aorist subjunctive φύgheTe ("how shall you escape") is grammatically a question but rhetorically a declaration — the subjunctive mood marks it as counterfactual, presupposing that escape is impossible. Jesus is not asking whether they can escape from hell's decree but affirming they cannot. The grammar itself closes the trap.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- **Double Vocative**: Both ὄφeιs and gennhmaTa are in the vocative case — the Pharisees are being directly addressed, summoned by name. The double vocative is a rhetorical escalation: first "serpents," then "offspring of vipers" — each naming deepens the condemnation.
- **Aorist Subjunctive**: The verb φύgheTe is parsed as V-ASA-2P — aorist subjunctive, second person plural. The subjunctive marks the question as counterfactual: the escape is presupposed to be impossible. This is not a genuine question but a rhetorical declaration of impossibility.
- **Genitive Chain**: The genitive chain ἐxiδnωv → τes krίseo_s → τes gehnnes creates a linked structure: the offspring *of vipers*, the judgment *of hell*. Each genitive tightens the grip — the Pharisees are caught between their parentage and their destination.

**Cultural/Contextual Notes**:
- The word gennhmaTa ("offspring") appears in Matthew 3:7 and 12:34 with the same construction: "brood of vipers." Jesus uses this formula consistently across his discourse to mark hereditary violence.
- The parallel to Revelation 12:9 ("the great dragon was cast out, that old serpent, called the Devil") shows Jesus's consistent association of the serpent with the Devil — to call the Pharisees serpents is to call them the Devil's offspring.
- The cross-reference to Hebrews 2:3 ("How shall we escape, if we neglect so great salvation") shows Jesus's use of the rhetorical "how shall you escape" as a recurring formula for impossibility.
