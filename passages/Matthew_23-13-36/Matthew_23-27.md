## 📖 Common English Translation (ESV)

> Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you are like whitewashed tombs, which outwardly appear beautiful, but within are full of dead people's bones and all uncleanness.

---

## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

*Original language nuances restored in readable English. Parenthetical notes explain the nuance recovered.*

Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you are like lime-washed tombs (the Greek verb κekoviaμév specifically means "covered with lime-wash" — a cheap coating over something ugly, not just generic "whitewashing"), which outwardly indeed (the Greek μὲv is a correlative conjunction meaning "indeed" — it sets up the contrast with the "however" that follows) appear beautiful, but within, however (the δὲ completes the correlative μὲv...δὲ structure: "indeed...however"), they are stuffed full of bones of the dead (the Greek is more compact and visceral — "bones of the dead" carries a funeral finality that "dead people's bones" loses) and of all impurity (the Greek ἀkaθaρsia is a privative — literally "absence of purity" — carrying deeper spiritual force than "uncleanness," which sounds merely dirty).

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

1. **The Tomb as Metaphor for the Pharisee**: The taphos (tomb/sepulcher) is not just any metaphor — it's a structure built for the dead, made to look beautiful on the outside. The Pharisee is being compared to a burial site: externally beautiful, internally filled with death and decay. The metaphor cuts deeper than the cup-and-dish of 23:25: this isn't just about dirty contents, it's about death itself residing within.

2. **Lime-Wash as Cheap Cover**: The verb κoviaδw (to lime-wash) appears only twice in the entire concordance — here and in Acts 23:3 ("you whitewashed wall"). In both cases, it describes a cheap, superficial coating over something structurally corrupt. The Pharisee's beauty isn't just a covering; it's a specifically cheap covering, like lime-wash over crumbling stone. The irony is that the covering costs less than the restoration would.

3. **Impurity as Spiritual Degradation**: Ἀkaθaρsia is used across the concordance for spiritual impurity that leads to lawlessness (Romans 6:19), sexual immorality (Galatians 5:19), and greed (Ephesians 4:19). It's not just "dirtiness" — it's the active degradation of spiritual purity. When paired with "bones of the dead," it suggests that the interior of the Pharisee is not just messy but spiritually dead and decaying.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- **Correlative μₑv...δₑ**: This is a classical Greek contrast structure: "outwardly indeed (μₑv) they appear beautiful, within however (δₑ) they are full." The ESV flattens this to a simple "but," losing the concessive "indeed" that acknowledges the external beauty as real before introducing the devastating contrast.
- **Resultative participle (RPM/P)**: The form κekoviaμevois is a resultative participle — a completed action that produced a state. The tombs aren't being whitewashed; they HAVE BEEN whitewashed. The coating is already on, presenting as a finished state.
- **Middle/passive φaiñoτai**: The verb φaivw in middle/passive means "to appear/seem" — it's not about being beautiful but about appearing beautiful. The grammar encodes the deception: the beauty is in the seeming, not the being.

**Cultural/Contextual Notes**:
- Acts 23:3 uses the same verb κoviaδw for "whitewashed wall" — both passages denounce superficial exteriors over corrupt interiors
- Luke 11:44 contains a parallel verse comparing Pharisees to graves that "are made beautiful above yet are full of corruption within"
- The tomb metaphor recurs in Matthew 27 (the grave of the body) and Matthew 28 (the sepulcher) — taphos is a theological category in Matthew's rhetoric, not just a physical structure

**Cross-References**:
- Acts 23:3 (whitewashed wall — same verb κoviaδw)
- Luke 11:44 (parallel tomb/grave metaphor for Pharisees)
- Romans 6:19 (akatharsia paired with anomia — impurity leading to lawlessness)
- Ephesians 4:19 (every kind of impurity with greediness)
