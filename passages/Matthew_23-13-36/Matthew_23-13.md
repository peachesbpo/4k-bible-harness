## 📊 Diff Analysis: What Got Lost?

**The ESV accurately captures the meaning of this verse. No significant translation loss detected.**

The ESV's "because" for Greek ᅅti (g3754) is a sound rendering — arguably superior to the KJV's "for," which loses the causal dimension. The ESV preserves both the causal relationship and the rhetorical force of the original.

---

## 📝 Etymological Notes (commentary, not translation loss)

- **ᅅti (g3754)**: The causal particle "that/because" — the ESV's "because" captures this well, arguably better than the KJV's "for." The Greek carries a sense of "in that" or "seeing that" which is slightly more explanatory than purely causal, but the ESV's rendering is defensible and accurate.
- **Οὐαὶ (g3759)**: "Woe" — a lamentation word in Greek, carrying the sense of a cry or exclamation of grief. The ESV's "woe" is an excellent equivalent, preserving both the archaic register and the emotional force.
- **graμμateῖs (g1122)**: "Scribes" — from the root for "letter-writer" or "scribe." In Jesus's context this refers to those who write letters (and thus can twist words), not merely copyists.
- **Φarisaῖoi (g5330)**: "Pharisees" — those who deal in interpretations and distinctions, from the Greek word play on "Pharisee" — Jesus is punning on those who make fine (pharisical) distinctions in Scripture to suit their own ends.
- **�upokritaί (g5273)**: "Hypocrites" — the Greek makes explicit what the scribes and Pharisees are: pretenders. Jesus groups all three together as variations of the same sin.
- **κλeiεte (g2808)**: "Shut" — the verb for closing or barring. Jesus uses this in the sense of shutting the kingdom against men — an active, deliberate barring of access.
- **βasilεiav (g932)**: "Kingdom" — from the root βasilεia, meaning kingdom or dominion. The "kingdom of heaven" is Jesus's image for the divine realm that should be open to all. *Imagine if this word were lost in the original manuscripts — the verse would read "ye shut up the ___ of heaven against men," losing the entire architectural metaphor. Jesus isn't saying they shut heaven itself, but that they shut the kingdom of heaven — the access point, the gateway.*
- **oujravῶv (g3772)**: "Of heaven" — the genitive linking the kingdom to heaven. The kingdom belongs to heaven, not to the scribes who would shut it.
- **εᰰseλtheiv (g1525)**: The root prose- (enter/approach) — the idea of entering in, which is thematically central: the Pharisees shut the kingdom but won't enter themselves, yet prevent others from entering.

---

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

This is the **first woe** — the foundational accusation. Jesus condemns those who shut the kingdom of heaven against others while refusing to enter it themselves. The Greek structure emphasizes the *hypocrisy* at the heart of the accusation: the same people who bar entry are themselves unwilling to enter. The "woe" (Οὐaὶ, g3759) is not merely "alas" but a cry of divine grief — Jesus mourns for the men shut out.

The ESV's "because" (vs. KJV's "for") correctly identifies the causal relationship: the woe falls *because* the kingdom is shut. This is not a parallel accusation but an explanatory one.

The Strong's number for "kingdom" (g932) points to βasilεia — a word Jesus uses deliberately to frame heaven as a *governed realm* with access points, not merely a spiritual concept. The scribes are not merely denying spiritual comfort; they are *shutting a kingdom*, an act of institutional barring. This architectural metaphor is the backbone of Jesus's entire rhetoric in Matthew 23.
