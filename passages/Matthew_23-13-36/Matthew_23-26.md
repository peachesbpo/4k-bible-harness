# Matthew 23:26 — 4K Verse Analysis

**Part of**: Matthew 23:13-36 (Seven Woes: Woes Against the Scribes and Pharisees)
**Original Language**: Greek (NA28/Nestle 1904)
**Analyst**: Peaches (4K Bible Translation Harness)

## 📖 Common English Translation (ESV)

> You blind Pharisee! First clean the inside of the cup and the plate, that the outside also may be clean.

---

## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

*Original language nuances restored in readable English. Parenthetical notes explain the nuance recovered.*

You blind (willfully blind — the blindness is moral, not physical; the Pharisee refuses to see) Pharisee! Cleanse (the imperative is aorist — sharp, urgent, commanding; and the verb means "purify," not just "clean") first the inside of the cup (the Nestle 1904 text does not include the dish here — other editions add "and of the dish," but the canonical Greek has only the cup), so that (the conjunction ἵνα introduces a purpose clause — a desired end, not a casual "that") the outside of it also might become (the subjunctive mood signals a wished-for possibility, not a guaranteed result) clean.

| 2 | "and the plate" | The Nestle 1904 Greek text does NOT include "and of the dish" (paρoψidos) in this verse. | 3 | "that" | The Greek ἵνα (hina) is a purpose conjunction — "so that / in order that" — introducing a final clause expressing the intended goal. | "That" in English can be causal, temporal, or purposive. ἵνα specifically marks purpose: "so that the outside might become clean also." The teleological force — cleaning the inside FOR THE SAKE OF the outside — is the entire moral point. |

The Pharisee is told to start inside because outside appearance follows from interior purity, not the reverse. |

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

1. **The Inversion Principle**: This verse is the constructive counterpart to 23:25's denunciation. Instead of "you clean the outside but the inside is foul," it commands: "cleanse first the inside, so that the outside also may become clean." The principle is that external righteousness follows from internal purity, never the reverse. The Pharisee's error is trying to work from outside in rather than inside out.

2. **Blindness as Moral Choice**: The word τyφlé (blind) appears 50 times in the Strong's concordance — it's a common metaphor, but here it's singular and direct: "You blind Pharisee!" The blindness is moral, not physical — the Pharisee is blind to the inside, blind to the necessity of starting from within. It's a willful blindness, which is why the command to cleanse follows.

3. **Subjunctive Humility**: The subjunctive γéνηtai ("might become") carries theological weight: interior purity is necessary but perhaps not sufficient for exterior beauty. Jesus isn't promising that cleaning the inside guarantees a clean outside — he's saying that without interior purity, exterior cleanliness is impossible. The grammar itself encodes a theology of inner-directed virtue.

## 🔍 Detailed Linguistic Analysis

**Greek Text** (Nestle 1904):
> Φαρισαῖε τυφλέ, καθαρισον πρῶτου τὸ ἐυτὸσ τοῦ ποτεριου ἵυα γευηται καὶ τὰ ἐκτὸσ αυτου καθαρου.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- **Aorist imperative vs. present indicative**: The command kaθaρisov is aorist imperative (V-AMA-2S) — a sharp, one-time command, not an ongoing suggestion. This contrasts with 23:25's present indicative kaθaρizete (you cleanse), which describes what they currently do. The shift from description (23:25) to command (23:26) is grammatically significant.
- **Subjunctive purpose clause**: The combination ἵva + γévetai (purpose conjunction + subjunctive verb) creates a classical Greek purpose construction — "so that it might become." This is not a casual result but a desired telos.
- **Textual variant on the dish**: The Nestle 1904 omits "καὶ τῆς paρoψidos" (and of the dish), while other editions include it. The BibleHub analysis marks these words with brackets (‹›), indicating they appear in some editions but not the Nestle.

**Cultural/Contextual Notes**:
- The parallel cleansing instruction appears in Matthew 12:33 ("Either make the tree good and his fruit good") and James 4:8 ("Cleanse your hands") — the principle of inside-out purification is a recurring theological theme
- The singular address (one Pharisee) after the plural denunciation of 23:25 (scribes and Pharisees) creates a moment of direct, personal confrontation
- The blindness metaphor connects to Matthew 11:5 ("The blind receive sight") — blindness and sight are theological categories in Matthew's rhetoric, not just physical states

**Cross-References**:
- Matthew 12:33 (make the tree good first, then the fruit)
- Isaiah 55:7 (the wicked forsaking his way)
- James 4:8 (cleanse your hands)
- Hebrews 10:22 (draw near with true heart)
