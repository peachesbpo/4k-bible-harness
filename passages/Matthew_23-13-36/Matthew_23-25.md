# Matthew 23:25 — 4K Verse Analysis

**Part of**: Matthew 23:13-36 (Seven Woes: Woes Against the Scribes and Pharisees)
**Original Language**: Greek (NA28/Nestle 1904)
**Analyst**: Peaches (4K Bible Translation Harness)

## 📖 Common English Translation (ESV)

> Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you clean the outside of the cup and the plate, but inside they are full of greed and self-indulgence.

---

## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

*Original language nuances restored in readable English. Parenthetical notes explain the nuance recovered.*

Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you cleanse (purify — the verb carries the sense of moral purification, not merely wiping) the outside of the cup and of the dish (a platter — a vessel for serving, not a flat plate), but inside they are stuffed (filled to capacity — the Greek verb implies being crammed full, not merely containing) with plunder (the seizing of what belongs to another — not just greed, but the act of robbery itself) and self-indulgence (literally "lack of self-restraint" — the failure to govern oneself).

## 📊 Diff Analysis: What Got Lost?

| # | Standard (ESV) | What's Lost | Why It Matters |
|---|----------------|-------------|----------------|
| 1 | "you clean" | The Greek verb καθαρίζετε means "cleanse/purify," not merely "clean." The root carries a moral-purification sense (κατα + ἁριζω). | The Pharisees aren't just wiping surfaces; they're performing a parody of purification — making the outside look morally clean while leaving the inside corrupt. The irony is sharper when the verb itself implies moral purification. |
| 2 | "plate" | The Greek παροψίδος means "dish/platter" — a curved serving vessel, not a flat plate. | The cup and platter are both vessels for serving food to others. This is about the tools of hospitality being cleaned on the outside while the contents remain corrupt — a metaphor for how Pharisees serve others. |
| 3 | "full of greed" | The Greek ἁρπαγῆς (Strong's 724, only 3 occurrences) means "plunder/robbery/extortion" — the violent act of seizing, not just the desire for more. | "Greed" is a disposition; "plunder" is an action. The Pharisees aren't just greedy — they're actively seizing what belongs to others. The moral indictment is about theft, not just appetite. |
| 4 | "they are full" | The Greek γέμουσιν implies "stuffed/crammed full" — a state of being filled to overflowing, not merely containing something. | The intensity matters: the inside isn't just occupied by corruption; it is saturated with it. There's no room for anything good because the bad fills every corner. |

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

1. **The Purification Parody**: The verb καθαρίζετε (to purify/cleanse) is the same root used for moral and spiritual purification throughout Matthew's gospel. The Pharisees perform a parody of genuine purification — they make the exterior look purified while the interior remains corrupt. This sets up the entire passage's critique of surface-level virtue.

2. **Plunder vs. Greed — The Act of Theft**: ἁρπαγῆς appears only 3 times in the entire concordance (Matthew 23:25, Luke 11:39, Hebrews 10:34) — always in the context of moral denunciation. The word is about seizure and robbery, not mere desire. The Pharisees are accused not of wanting too much, but of actively taking what isn't theirs — a more serious moral charge.

3. **Self-Indulgence as Incontinence**: ἀκρασία (Strong's 192, only 2 occurrences) is translated as "self-indulgence" but literally means "incontinence" or "lack of self-restraint" — the inability to control oneself. The Pharisees aren't just indulging; they lack the internal discipline to restrain themselves at all.

## 🔍 Detailed Linguistic Analysis

**Greek Text** (Nestle 1904):
> Οὐαὶ ὑμῖν, γραμματεῖς καὶ Φαρισαῖοι ὑποκριταί, ὅτι καθαρίζετε τὸ ἔξωθεν τοῦ ποτηρίου καὶ τῆς παροψίδος, ἔσωθεν δὲ γέμουσιν ἐξ ἁρπαγῆς καὶ ἀκρασίας.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- **Vocative triad**: The three nouns (scribes, Pharisees, hypocrites) are all in the vocative case — direct address, not description. Michael is calling out these groups by name, not merely labeling them.
- **Correlative ἔξwθev...ἔσwθev δέ**: The contrast between outside and inside is structurally emphasized by the correlative adverbs and the adversative δέ ("however"). The ESV flattens this to a simple "but."
- **ὅti (hoti) as "For"**: The conjunction ὅti introduces an explanatory clause — "For you cleanse..." — providing the justification for the "Woe." It's causal, not just a filler.

**Cultural/Contextual Notes**:
- The cup and dish (ποteρiou καὶ paρoψidos) are vessels for serving food to others — the metaphor targets Pharisees who serve others while being internally corrupt
- Luke 11:39-40 contains a parallel verse with the same metaphor, suggesting this was a standard image in Matthew's rhetorical toolkit
- The Byzantine Majority Text (2005) has ἀdikias ("advice/injustice") instead of ἀkraisas — a textual variant that changes the specific charge from self-indulgence to injustice

**Cross-References**:
- Matthew 15:19-20 (evil proceeding from the heart)
- Mark 7:4 (washing before eating from the market)
- Luke 11:39-40 (parallel cup-and-dish metaphor)
- Acts 23:3 (whitewashed wall — same conceit of surface beauty over internal corruption)
