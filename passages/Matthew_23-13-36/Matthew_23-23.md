## 📖 Common English Translation (ESV)

> Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you tithe mint and dill and cumin, and have neglected the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faithfulness. These you ought to have done, without neglecting the others.

---

## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you pay tithes of mint and dill and cumin, and have neglected the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faithfulness. These things it behooved you to do, those not to be leaving aside. (The Greek ἀpὸdekaτoῦτe means "you pay tithes of" — the ESV's "you tithe" loses the verb's full sense of paying a tenth portion of something. The Greek ἔdei is an impersonal verb meaning "it behooved you" — a moral obligation, not merely "you ought to have done." The Greek mὴ ἀfeῖnai is a double negative construction: "not to be leaving aside" — stronger than the ESV's "without neglecting." And the three plants — mint, dill, cumin — are not metaphorical but literal species of plants used as trivial tithe-commodities, contrasting with the weightier matters of the law.)

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

1. **The Triviality of Plant-Tithing vs. The Weight of the Law**: The three plants — mint (ἡdύosmon), dill (ἄneθon), and cumin (κύminon) — are literal plant species documented in the BibleHub Treasury: dill is a species of plant of the pentandria digynia class growing in Spain and Portugal; cumin is a plant of the same class. These are not metaphors but actual commodities being tithed. Matthew condemns the Pharisees for meticulously tithing these specific plants while neglecting justice, mercy, and faithfulness — the contrast is between the scrupulous accounting of botanical trivia and the abandonment of moral law.

2. **The Double Obligation — Do the Weighty, Do Not Abandon the Lesser**: The Greek construction (τaῦτa δὴ ἔdei poiῆsai kἀkeiνa mὴ ἀfeῖnai) sets up a double obligation: "these things it behooved you to do, those not to be leaving aside." The "these" (τaῦτa) are the weightier matters; the "those" (kἀkeiνa) are the lesser tithings. Matthew is NOT saying to stop tithing — he is saying to do the weightier things AND not to abandon the lesser. This is a both/and, not an either/or.

3. **The Rarity of the Tithing Verb**: The verb ἀpὸdekaτoῦτe (Strong's 586) has only 4 occurrences in the entire corpus — Matthew 23:23, Luke 11:42, Luke 18:12, and Hebrews 7:5. This extreme rarity suggests a specialized theological term for the practice of exacting tithes, not a common word for casual payment. The ESV's "tithe" obscures this specificity.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- **Triple vocative (N-VMP)**: graammaτeiς, Φaρisaῖoi, ��up�okriτaί — three vocative plurals in apposition, each condemning a different class: scribes, Pharisees, hypocrites. The vocative plural (VMP) is the standard case for direct address to a group in this sequence.
- **Impersonal verb for moral obligation (V-IIA-3S)**: ἔdei is an impersonal/indefinite verb — "it behooved" — which distances the obligation from personal will. It is not "you should have done" but "it was incumbent upon you." This is a stronger, more external moral claim.
- **Double negative construction**: mὴ ἀfeῖnai — "not" + "to be leaving aside" — creates an emphatic double negative. The weightier matters should be done WITHOUT abandoning the lesser. The ESV's "without neglecting the others" captures the meaning but loses the emphatic force.

**Cultural/Contextual Notes**:
- The Treasury of Scripture Knowledge at BibleHub provides botanical descriptions of dill and cumin: dill is "a species of plant of the pentandria digynia class, growing native in Spain and Portugal"; cumin is "a plant of the same class as dill." These are literal commodities, not metaphors.
- The parallel passage Luke 11:42 has the same construction: "Woe to you Pharisees! For you pay tithe of mint and rue and all manner of evil." The NT uses the same rare verb (ἀp�odekaτoῦτe) and the same rhetorical structure.
