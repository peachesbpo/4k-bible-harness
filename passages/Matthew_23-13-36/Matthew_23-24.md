## 📖 Common English Translation (ESV)

> You blind guides, straining out a gnat and swallowing a camel!

---

## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

Guides, blind! Those straining out a gnat, the camel however swallowing! (The Greek uses the vocative case for "guides" and "blind" — addressing them directly, not describing them. The word diϋliζonτes [Strong's 1368, 1 occurrence only] means "straining out" — the act of filtering or sifting — and is unique to this verse in the entire corpus. The word kὸnωpa [Strong's 2971, 1 occurrence only] for "gnat" is similarly unique. The Greek kaτapinonτes [Strong's 2666] means "swallowing" — but it is the participle form, actively swallowing, not the static "and swallow." The δὴ "however" before kaμeλon creates a contrastive emphasis: they strain out a tiny gnat, BUT a camel they swallow. The ESV loses this adversative "however," which is the rhetorical core of the image.)

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

1. **The Gnat and the Camel as Scale-Distortion Image**: The hypocrite's moral distortion is captured in a single image: they meticulously strain out a gnat (the tiniest particle, representing the trivial tithes of mint, dill, and cumin from 23:23) while swallowing a camel whole (the enormous moral failures — neglecting justice, mercy, and faithfulness). The gnat represents the painstaking precision of the Pharisee's trivial tithing; the camel represents the gross moral negligence that they consume without noticing. The image is about the inversion of moral scale.

2. **The Vocative as Direct Condemnation**: The Greek uses the vocative case for �idigoi "guides" and τypλoi "blind" — directly addressing the hypocrites as "guides, blind!" rather than descriptively saying "you blind guides." The vocative makes this a direct condemnation, not a characterization. Matthew is not describing them but naming them — calling out their blindness as their identity.

3. **The Extreme Rarity of Key Terms**: Both diϋliζonτes (Strong's 1368, "straining out") and kὸnωpa (Strong's 2971, "gnat") appear exactly once in the entire NT corpus. This extreme rarity suggests that Matthew chose these words for their precise image — the act of filtering and the tiny unit of weight — to make a point that could not be made with common vocabulary. The uniqueness of the words matches the uniqueness of the moral distortion they describe.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- **Double vocative (N-VMS + Adj-VMP)**: �idigoi "guides" (noun vocative) + τypλoi "blind" (adjective vocative) — a double vocative construction that addresses the hypocrites directly, naming them as both "guides" and "blind" in the same breath. The blindness is not a quality but an identity.
- **Unique verb and noun pairing**: diϋliζonτes (Strong's 1368, 1 occurrence) and kὸnωpa (Strong's 2971, 1 occurrence) are both unique to this verse. This pairing of two hapax legomena (words appearing only once) in a single verse is remarkable and suggests deliberate word choice for precision of image.
- **Contrastive conjunction before the contrasted noun**: The placement of δὴ "however" before kaμeλon "camel" (rather than before the verb) creates a contrastive emphasis on the noun itself — it is the CAMEL that is the point, not the swallowing. The hypocrite swallows the camel; the gnat they strain out.

**Cultural/Contextual Notes**:
- The gnat-and-camel image is one of the most famous in the NT, comparable to Matthew 19:24 ("easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle") and Matthew 7:4 ("pull out the mote out of the beam"). Both use extreme physical scale-contrasts to illustrate moral distortion.
- The cross-references for this verse include Matthew 15:2-6 (disciples transgressing tradition), Matthew 19:24 (camel through needle's eye), Matthew 27:6-8 (chief priests taking silver pieces), Luke 6:7-10 (scribes and Pharisees watching), and John 18:28,40 (led from Caiaphas to hall of judgment) — all scenes of hypocritical judgment and moral blindness.
- The ESV's "straining out a gnat" captures the image but loses the adversative δὴ "however" that makes the camel-swallowing a contrastive condemnation, not merely a parallel action.
- The Greek Orthodox Church edition adds an exclamation mark after kaτapinonτes — "swallowing!" — which matches the vocative force of the construction. The Nestle 1904 does not have the exclamation mark, but the vocative/condemnatory force is implicit in the grammar.

**Cross-References**:

- Matthew 15:14 — "they are blind guides of the blind"
- Matthew 19:24 — "easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle"
- Matthew 23:16 — "Woe to you, blind guides, who say..." (parallel construction)
- Luke 6:7-10 — "The scribes and Pharisees watched him, whether he would heal..."
