## 📊 Diff Analysis: What Got Lost?

**The ESV accurately captures this verse with no significant translation loss.**

The ESV is actually *more* faithful to the Greek than the KJV here:

1. **"Debtor" consistency**: The Greek word oφeiλi is the same in both 23:16 and 23:18. The ESV correctly renders both as "debtor," preserving Jesus's deliberate parallelism. The KJV inconsistently switches to "guilty" in 23:18, losing the financial-debt metaphor.
2. **"Shall swear" accuracy**: The Greek 👣av 👩μmσΏ uses an auxiliary + subjunctive construction meaning "shall swear." The ESV captures this accurately; the KJV's "sweareth" loses the auxiliary "shall" (future obligation).

These are ESV strengths, not ESV losses. See Etymological Notes for details.

## 📝 Etymological Notes (commentary, not translation loss)

- **Kai (Kai)**: "And" — Jesus opens with "and" to signal this verse is a continuation of 23:16's pattern. The altar/gift contrast mirrors the temple/gold contrast exactly.
- **θusiasτεria (thusiasteria)**: "Altar" — the sacred place of offering. Just as the temple sanctified the gold (23:16-17), the altar sanctifies the gift. Jesus is extending the metaphor: altar : gift :: temple : gold.
- **δopa (dopa)**: "Gift" — the material offering placed upon the altar. The gift is analogous to the gold in 23:16 — it is the *content* that the sacred *form* (altar) gives meaning to.
- **τa eπavo aitot (ta epano aiton)**: "That is upon it" — the gift sits upon the altar, just as the gold sat within the temple. The spatial metaphor is consistent: the sacred structure contains/supports the material content.
- **oφeiλei (opheilei)**: "A debtor" — the same Greek word used in 23:16. Jesus is deliberately repeating the financial metaphor: swearing by the material rather than the sacred makes you a *debtor* — spiritually bankrupt. The ESV preserves this consistency; the KJV breaks it with "guilty."

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

This is the **sixth woe** — Jesus extends the temple/gold metaphor to a new domain: altar and gift. The pattern is identical: swearing by the sacred structure (altar) is "nothing" (innocent), but swearing by the material content (gift) makes one a "debtor" (spiritually bankrupt).

The theological insight is Jesus's systematic demolition of material-over-spiritual valuation. In 23:16-17, he established that the temple sanctifies the gold (not vice versa). In 23:18, he extends the principle: the altar sanctifies the gift. To swear by the gift rather than the altar is to mistake the *contained* for the *container* — to value what is upon the altar more than the altar itself.

The consistent use of oφeiλei ("debtor") in both 23:16 and 23:18 is deliberate. Jesus is saying that spiritual debt — owing what you cannot repay — is the *inevitable* consequence of valuing material over sacred. It is not a one-time error but a recurring category of sin. The ESV preserves this consistency faithfully; the KJV's switch to "guilty" in 23:18 obscures Jesus's deliberate parallelism.

The altar/gift contrast also connects to Jesus's broader theology of offering: the altar is where offerings are made, where sacrifice occurs. To swear by the *gift* (what is given) rather than the *altar* (where giving happens) is to invert the sacred economy — valuing the transaction over the sacred space that makes it meaningful.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- The parallel construction with 23:16 is exact: ᅦts γav ᅦμoσὐ ev τa [sacred thing]... ᅦts δὐ γav ᅦμoσὐ ev τa [material thing]... oφeiλei. Jesus is using identical syntax to create a parallel argument.
- The Greek word oφeiλei is identical in both 23:16 and 23:18 — confirming that Jesus intended the same meaning ("debtor") in both verses. The ESV preserves this; the KJV does not.
- The relative clause τa eπavo aitot ("that is upon it") uses spatial language — the gift is *upon* the altar — just as 23:16 used genitive construction for the gold *of* the temple. The spatial relationship differs (upon vs. of) but the metaphorical relationship is the same: material content supported by sacred structure.

**Cross-References**:
- This verse is the direct parallel of 23:16, with altar/gift replacing temple/gold
- The "debtor" (oφeiλei) in both 23:16 and 23:18 establishes a unified theological principle: spiritual debt results from valuing material over sacred
- The altar/gift contrast connects to Jesus's theology of sacrifice and offering — the altar is the sacred space, the gift is what is offered within it
- Together, 23:16-23:18 form a coherent sub-argument within the Seven Woes: the blind guides are condemned for inverting the sacred-material hierarchy, first with temple/gold and then with altar/gift
