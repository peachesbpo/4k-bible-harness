## 📖 Common English Translation (ESV)

> Ye blind guides, that strain out the gnat, and swallow the camel!

---

## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

*Original language nuances restored in readable English. Parenthetical notes explain the nuance recovered.*

Ye blind (and "blind" means opaque, as if smoky — clouded vision, not just of the eyes but of the mind and judgment) guides (and "guides" means conductors — literally "ones who lead on the path," from the Greek for "path" and "to lead"; Jesus has just spent verses 8-12 establishing who may lead, and these people are not it), that strain out (and "strain" means to filter, to examine with obsessive precision) the gnat (the tiniest thing — a trifle, a speck), and swallow (and "swallow" means to gulp down whole — to drink entire in one mouthful, to consume completely) the camel (the enormous thing — and the LSJ classical lexicon cites this very passage as the proverbial example: "camel swallow" = this verse)!

### Concepts Flattened

- **Mental blindness**: Flattened from "opaque, as if smoky" (clouded vision of mind and judgment) to simple "blind" (unable to see).
- **Guide as path-leader**: Flattened from "one who leads on the path" (with the connection to vv8-12 about who may lead) to a generic "guide."
- **Strain as meticulous filtering**: Flattened from "filter, examine with obsessive precision" to "strain" (which in modern English can mean merely "to try hard").
- **Swallow as gulping whole**: Flattened from "gulp down entire, consume completely" to "swallow" (which can mean merely "to eat").
- **The proverbial camel**: The LSJ marks "camel swallow" as a proverb, with this verse as its canonical citation. ESV readers see a metaphor but not a recognized proverb.

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

1. **"Blind" as mental, not just physical**: The Greek τuφlós means "opaque, as if smoky" — from the root for smoke. The LSJ shows the word used metaphorically for blindness of ALL the senses and the mind: "τuφlὸs τα τ' ωta, tov te voūv, taτ' Σμat' εi" — blind in the ears, in the nose, in the eyes. Jesus isn't saying these people can't see with their eyes. He's saying they can't perceive at all — their judgment is clouded, their vision smoky, every sense obscured. They are blind in their MINDS, not just their eyes.

2. **"Guides" connects to the instructor prohibition of vv8-12**: The Greek ὁδηγós derives from ὁδos (path/road) + �ηgēomai (to lead/conduct) — literally "one who leads on the path." Jesus just spent vv8-12 establishing who may lead: the Christ alone is instructor, the one who has walked the path. Now he calls these people "guides" — those who presume to lead others — and calls them BLIND. The connection is deliberate: you cannot lead others on a path you cannot see. The Pharisees are blind guides leading the blind.

3. **The strain/swallow contrast IS the theology**: The two participles — δiüλizoνtes (straining) and kaτapivoνtes (swallowing) — form a deliberate contrast. Both modify the same blind guides. The Pharisees STRAIN at the gnat (filter with obsessive precision, examining every grain of the tiniest thing) and SWALLOW the camel (gulp down the enormous thing whole, without examination). The contrast encodes the priority inversion: meticulous about small things, oblivious about great ones. That IS their blindness — not that they can't see, but that they choose to see only the small things.

4. **The camel proverb**: The LSJ classical lexicon cites this very passage as the proverbial example: "prov. kaēmēlon kaτapivēn Ev.Matt.23.24." The expression "to swallow the camel" was a recognized proverb in classical Greek — and Jesus is using it as a proverb, a known image, not inventing a new metaphor. The Pharisees' sin is not just described but NAMED — it's a recognized pattern of behavior so common it has a proverb.

5. **"Blind guides" as a contradiction in terms**: A guide who is blind is a contradiction — one who leads others on a path they cannot see. Jesus places the contradiction front and center: these people presume to guide others while lacking the vision to guide anyone, including themselves. The contradiction IS the indictment.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- **Two participles modifying "blind guides"**: Both δiüλizoνtes (straining) and kaτapivoνtes (swallowing) are participles in the nominative plural, modifying the subject "blind guides." The Pharisees do BOTH — they strain AND swallow. Both actions belong to the same people. The grammatical parallel makes the contrast deliberate: the same people who strain at the gnat swallow the camel.
- **Adverbial conjunction δὴ (de)**: The δὴ between the two participles is ADDITIVE, not contrastive — "and/moreover." Jesus isn't saying "they strain at the gnat BUT swallow the camel" (contrast). He's saying "they strain at the gnat AND MOREOVER swallow the camel" (addition). Both actions are true simultaneously. The additivity makes the indictment worse: they do both things at once.
- **Etymology of τuφlós (blind)**: From τuφōω (to smoke, be smoky). The blindness isn't just absence of vision but the PRESENCE of obscuration — something smoky, cloudy, opaque is in the way. The Pharisees' vision isn't empty; it's CLOUDED. Something is in front of their eyes.
- **Etymology of ὁδηγós (guide)**: From ὁδos (path/road) + �ηgēomai (to lead/conduct). A guide is literally "one who leads on the path." The word connects directly to vv8-12 where Jesus established who may lead (the Christ alone is instructor). These blind guides are leading where they cannot see.
- **Proverbial usage of kaēmēlon kaτapivō**: The LSJ marks this as a proverb: "prov. kaēmēlon kaτapivēn Ev.Matt.23.24." Jesus is using a recognized classical Greek proverb, not inventing a new metaphor. The pattern — straining at small things while swallowing large ones — was a known behavior so common it earned a proverb.

**Cultural/Contextual Notes**:
- This verse is the climactic image of the Seven Woes pericope. After the specific indictment of v23 (you tithe trifles and omit the weightier matters), v24 sharpens the critique into a single image: blind guides who strain at a gnat and swallow a camel. The image captures the priority inversion of v23 in visual form.
- The camel reappears in Matthew 19:24 (cross-reference, 9 votes): "It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of God." There the camel is the thing too large to fit through a small opening; here it's the thing swallowed whole while straining at a gnat. Both uses share the sense of disproportionate scale.
- The word kaēmēlos (camel) is noted as being of Hebrew origin (cf. Hebr. gāmāl) — like Φaρισaῖos (Pharisee), it has Hebrew roots. The Pharisees, who strain at the gnat and swallow the camel, are named with a Hebrew-origin word, and the thing they swallow is also a Hebrew-origin word.

**Cross-References**:
- **Matthew 19:24** (9 votes) — "It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of God." — The camel reappears as an image of disproportionate scale. There: a camel too large for a needle's eye. Here: a camel swallowed whole while straining at a gnat. Both use the camel as the measure of what is enormous.
