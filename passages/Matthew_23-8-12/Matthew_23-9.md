## 📖 Common English Translation (ESV)

> And call no man your father on earth, for you have one Father, who is in heaven.

---

## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

*Original language nuances restored in readable English. Parenthetical notes explain the nuance recovered.*

And do not call anyone on earth your father (Greek emphasizes "FATHER" first — this is the title being prohibited, not just the word), for you have one Father (the same word, but now as the supreme subject — THE Father, not merely a title), who is heavenly (not just "in heaven" as a location, but *heavenly* — carrying heaven's purity, permanence, and authority in his very nature).

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

1. **"Heavenly" as a quality, not a location — the Father IS heaven's nature**: oupavios (G3770) is an ADJECTIVE, not a prepositional phrase. The Father is not merely "in heaven" (a location) but "heavenly" (a quality). BibleHub's Topical Lexicon: "By calling something 'heavenly,' the writers identify it as proceeding from that realm, sharing its purity and permanence, and therefore carrying absolute authority." The Father is HEAVENLY — He partakes of heaven's purity, heaven's permanence, heaven's authority. This is not just WHERE He is but WHAT HE IS.

**Grammatical Observations**:
- **Object-first word order (paτera μὴ calesete)**: Greek places the OBJECT before the verb for emphasis. "FATHER — no one — call!" not "call no one father." The emphasis falls on "FATHER" — the title being prohibited. ESV's "call no man your father" buries the key word. Greek front-loads it: FATHER is what you must not call anyone.
- **Same verb (kaleō, G2564) across vv7-9**: kaleō appears in v7 (Pharisees want to be CALLED Rabbi), v8 (disciples shall not be CALLED Rabbi), and v9 (call NO ONE father). The same verb of naming/inviting runs through all three verses, creating a rhetorical thread: the Pharisees want to be called, Jesus prohibits his disciples from being called, and Jesus prohibits his disciples from calling anyone else by a divine title.
- **paτer / Paτer — same word, different case**: The word for "father" appears twice — first as accusative (paτera, the title you must not confer), then as nominative (Paτer, the unique Father who is). The grammatical shift from object to subject enacts the theological shift: any earthly "father" is merely an object (a title), but THE Father is the SUBJECT — the one who IS, who acts, who dwells in heaven.
- **oupavios (G3770) — "heavenly" not "in heaven"**: ESV renders this as "who is in heaven," but the Greek is an ADJECTIVE, not a prepositional phrase. oupavios means "heavenly" — it's a quality of the Father, not just a location. The Father is HEAVENLY — He partakes of heaven's nature, heaven's purity, heaven's authority. It's not just where He is but WHAT HE IS. BibleHub's Topical Lexicon: by calling something "heavenly," the writers identify it as "proceeding from that realm, sharing its purity and permanence, and therefore carrying absolute authority."

**Cultural/Contextual Notes**:
- **Father as divine title**: In the OT, God is Father of Israel (Deuteronomy 32:6, Isaiah 63:16). Jesus teaches his disciples to address God as "Our Father in heaven" (Matthew 6:9). The title "Father" belongs to GOD alone — not to any earthly teacher, leader, or mentor. Jesus forbids his disciples from calling anyone on earth "father" because there is ONE Father, and He dwells in heaven.
- **The earthly realm vs. the heavenly realm**: The contrast is between ἐpὶ τῆs γῆs ("on the earth") and oupavios ("heavenly"). The earth is the mortal, physical realm; heaven is the divine, eternal realm. No one ON EARTH can be your Father, because the one Father is HEAVENLY — He dwells in and partakes of the divine realm.
- **Matthew 6:9 connection**: Jesus teaches the Lord's Prayer: "Our Father, who is in heaven." Verse 9 echoes this exactly — the same construction (ὁ Paτer ὁ oupavios) identifies the one Father as heavenly, just as the Lord's Prayer does.
