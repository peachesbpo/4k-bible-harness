## 📖 Common English Translation (ESV)

> Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye cleanse the outside of the cup and of the platter, but within they are full from extortion and excess.

---

## ✨ 4K Expanded Translation

*Original language nuances restored in readable English. Parenthetical notes explain the nuance recovered.*

Woe unto you (an exclamation of pain and anger — not just "alas" but the cry of grief itself), scribes (and "scribes" means secretaries, registrars, subordinate officials — a word sometimes used as a term of contempt) and Pharisees (and "Pharisees" means separatists — those who have made themselves exclusively religious, a sect apart), hypocrites (and "hypocrites" literally means stage-players — actors under an assumed character, performing a role rather than living a truth)! For ye cleanse (and "cleanse" is a purification verb — the same word used for cleansing from leprosy, cleansing the conscience, purifying from stains; its semantic category is "purity rite," not just "wash") the outside (and "the outside" means externally — from without, the exterior surface, opposed to the inside) of the cup (and "cup" means a drinking-vessel, but by extension a cupful or draught, and figuratively a LOT or FATE — the cup is a metaphor for one's portion in life) and of the platter (and "platter" means a side-dish, the receptacle — but the classical lexicon shows it used metaphorically for "fresh tastes of misery," a source of bitter portions), but within (and "within" is the precise opposite of "the outside" — from inside, the interior) they (the cup and platter) are full (and "full" means to swell out, to be full — and the classical lexicon cites THIS VERSE for the construction "full from," meaning full BY ORIGIN OF) from (and "from" is the preposition of ORIGIN — denoting the point whence something proceeds; extortion and excess are the SOURCE of the fullness, not merely its contents) extortion (and "extortion" means pillage — seizure, robbery, rape, taking by force; not overcharging but violent taking, rifling) and excess (and "excess" means want of self-restraint — not "too much" but an inability to control oneself, an incontinence, a lack of self-governance)!

### Concepts Flattened

- **Purification rite reduced to washing**: "Cleanse" (kaθaɾιze) is a purification verb with moral and spiritual connotations — the same word used for cleansing from leprosy and purifying the conscience. ESV's "cleanse" captures the literal sense but loses the ritual force. The Pharisees perform a PURIFICATION RITE on the outside only.
- **Cup as lot/fate reduced to literal vessel**: "Cup" (poτήrion) has three semantic levels — literal vessel → cupful/draught → lot/fate. ESV captures only the literal. The figurative sense transforms the verse: the Pharisees cleanse the exterior of their LOT while the interior of their LOT is full of robbery.
- **Platter as source of misery reduced to literal dish**: "Platter" (paρoψίdos) has a metaphorical sense in the LSJ — "fresh tastes of misery." The platter dispenses bitterness, and the Pharisees cleanse its outside.
- **"Full from" reduced to "full from"**: The Greek construction γέmw ἐξ (be full from/out of) is cited in the LSJ with this verse. The preposition ἐξ denotes ORIGIN — extortion is the SOURCE of the fullness, not merely its contents. The distinction is between "full of" (contents) and "full from" (origin).
- **Extortion as robbery reduced to overcharging**: The Greek ἁρpagῆ means pillage, seizure, robbery — violent taking. Modern "extortion" suggests overcharging, a milder sin. The cup's inside is full from ROBBERY.
- **Excess as incontinence reduced to "too much"**: The Greek ἀkρasia means want of self-restraint — an inability to govern oneself. Modern "excess" suggests abundance. The cup's inside is full from INCONTINENCE, a failure of self-control.

## 💡 Theological Insights Recovered

1. **The cup as metaphor for life itself**: The Greek poτήrion (cup) has a figurative sense: "a lot or fate." The cup isn't just a vessel — it's a metaphor for one's portion in life, one's lot. When Jesus says the Pharisees cleanse the outside of the cup while the inside is full from robbery, he's saying: you make your outward lot appear clean while your inward reality is full of violence. The cup is your LIFE — and you're only cleaning its surface.

2. **"Cleanse" as purification rite**: The semantic category of kaθaɾιze is `ritual>purity_rite` — this is a PURIFICATION verb, not just a washing verb. The LSJ shows it used for cleansing from leprosy, cleansing the conscience of dead things, purifying from stains, keeping a precinct clear. Jesus charges the Pharisees with performing the RITUAL of purification — but only on the outside. They go through the motions of purity without the substance. The ritual without the substance IS the hypocrisy.

3. **The outside/inside opposition as theological image**: The Greek uses ἔξωθen (outside) and ἔswωθen (within) — a paired opposition the LSJ explicitly marks as a known classical construction. The spatial metaphor IS the theology: the Pharisees cleanse the exterior while the interior is full from sin. The gap between surface and substance IS the hypocrisy — and it connects directly to v3's "hypocrites" (stage-players, actors performing a character). The actor performs the outside; the inside is full from robbery.

4. **"Full from" as origin, not contents**: The LSJ cites this verse for the construction γέmw ἐξ ("be full from/out of"). The preposition ἐξ denotes ORIGIN — the point whence something proceeds. Not "full of" (extortion as contents) but "full FROM" (extortion as origin/source). The distinction matters: the inside doesn't just contain robbery; robbery is its FOUNDATION, its starting point. Extortion is where the fullness comes from. The cup's interior isn't filled with robbery; it's full FROM robbery — robbery is its basis.

5. **Extortion as robbery and violence**: The Greek ἁρpagῆ means pillage, seizure, robbery, rape — taking by force. The LSJ shows it means BOOTY and PREY as well as the ACT of seizing. The categories are `action>destroy_violence` and `morality_spirituality>sin_evil`. This isn't "extortion" as overcharging — it's ROBBERY, violent taking, rifling. The cup's inside is full from VIOLENT SEIZURE. The word carries the force of destruction and violation.

6. **Excess as incontinence and failure of self-governance**: The Greek ἀkρasia means "want of self-restraint" — not abundance but INCONTINENCE. The primary category is `emotion_mind>attitude_character` — this is about CHARACTER, not quantity. The secondary category is `morality_spirituality>sin_evil`. The cup's inside is full from the inability to restrain oneself — a failure of self-governance, not an excess of abundance. The Pharisees cannot GOVERN themselves. They lack the very self-control that Jesus has been modeling throughout the chapter.

7. **Paired sins: commission and omission**: "Extortion" (pillage — taking by force, a sin of COMMISSION) and "excess" (incontinence — failing to restrain oneself, a sin of OMISSION) form a complete moral failure. Together they cover both forms of moral transgression: active violence (doing what you shouldn't) and passive indulgence (failing to do what you should). The cup's inside is full from BOTH — robbery AND incontinence. The pairing is deliberate: the Pharisees fail in both directions simultaneously.

8. **The return of scribes/Pharisees/hypocrites**: Same three words from v1, repeated in v23, now in v25. Jesus returns to the same targets with each woe. But each woe adds a new indictment: v1 = general condemnation, v23 = you tithe trifles and omit justice, v25 = you cleanse the outside while the inside is full of robbery and incontinence. The pattern is cumulative: each woe sharpens the critique. The cup and platter image adds the SPATIAL dimension — the hypocrisy isn't just about what they do (tithe trifles, omit justice) but about WHERE they do it (outside vs. inside, surface vs. substance).

**Grammatical Observations**:
- **Outside/inside opposition**: Jesus uses ἔξωθen (outside) and ἔswωθen (within) — a paired opposition the LSJ explicitly marks as a known classical construction. The LSJ defines ἔξωθen as "matters outside the house, opp. ἔswωθen." Jesus uses this exact opposition: ye cleanse the OUTSIDE but WITHIN they are full from sin. The spatial contrast IS the theological critique.
- **Adversative δὲ (but)**: The δὲ between the outside-clause and the inside-clause is adversative — "but." The LSJ shows δὲ as answering to μὲn, marking the second element of an opposition. Jesus places the adversative between outside and inside: outside ye cleanse, BUT inside they are full from sin. The grammar encodes the theological contrast.
- **Verb voice: active "ye cleanse" vs. passive "they are full"**: The outside-clause uses the active voice (kaθaɾιzeτe — "ye cleanse") — the Pharisees are the agents of cleansing. The inside-clause uses a stative verb (γéμoysiν — "they are full") — the cup and platter are in a state of fullness. The Pharisees ACT on the outside; the inside simply IS. The Pharisees control the surface; the interior has its own reality.
- **"Full from" (γέmw ἐξ) construction**: The LSJ cites this verse for the construction γéμw ἐξ ("be full from/out of"). The preposition ἐξ denotes ORIGIN — the point whence something proceeds. Not "full of" (contents) but "full FROM" (origin). Robbery and incontinence are the SOURCE of the fullness, not merely what fills the vessel. The distinction is between container and foundation.
- **Etymology of kaθaɾιze (cleanse)**: From kaθaρos (clean/pure) + -ize (factitive verb-forming suffix). The LSJ shows it used for cleansing from leprosy (Ev.Matt.8.2), cleansing the conscience of dead things, purifying from stains, keeping a precinct clear. The semantic category `ritual>purity_rite` marks this as a PURIFICATION verb — not just washing but performing a rite of purification.
- **Etymology of paρoψis (platter)**: From paρa (beside/side) + ὁψaρion (receptacle). The LSJ shows a metaphorical sense: "τῶν kakῶn paρoψides fresh tastes of misery" — the platter as a source of bitter portions. The word isn't neutral — it carries the connotation of dispensing bitterness.
- **Etymology of ἁρpagῆ (extortion)**: From ἁρpaζw (to seize, to grab). The LSJ shows the noun means seizure, robbery, rape, booty, prey, greediness. The progression from verb (to seize) to noun (seizure, booty) shows the word encompasses both the ACT and the RESULT. The cup's inside is full from both the ACT of seizing and the BOOTY of seizure.

**Cultural/Contextual Notes**:
- This verse continues the Seven Woes pericope (vv1, 13, 14, 23, 25, 27, 28). Jesus returns to the same targets — scribes, Pharisees, hypocrites — with each woe, adding a new indictment. V25 adds the SPATIAL dimension: the hypocrisy isn't just about what they do but WHERE they do it (outside vs. inside, surface vs. substance).
- The cup and platter are vessels from the Eucharist context (the "cup" in the Eucharist, per LSJ). Jesus repurposes these Eucharistic vessels as metaphors for the Pharisees' moral state: clean on the outside, full of robbery and incontinence on the inside.
- The word poτήrion (cup) appears in Matthew 22:48 ("consider the lilies...") and in the Eucharist context. Its figurative sense — "a lot or fate" — connects to Jesus' broader theme of one's lot in life being determined by what one carries within.
- The "outside/inside" opposition connects to Jesus' broader critique throughout Matthew 23 of the difference between external conformity and internal reality. The Pharisees perform the outside of purity while harboring the inside of sin. The image captures Jesus' central theological claim: what matters is not the surface but the substance.

**Cross-References**:
- **Matthew 23:1** (Seven Woes pericope) — The first "Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!" — the general condemnation. V25 returns to the same targets with the specific indictment of outside/inside hypocrisy.
- **Matthew 23:3** (hypocrites) — "Woe unto you... hypocrites!" — the term ὑpoκritaί (stage-players, actors under an assumed character) is the same word. The actor performs the OUTSIDE; the INSIDE is full from robbery. The theatrical metaphor of v3 finds its spatial expression in v25.
- **Matthew 23:23** (tithe and omit) — The previous woe: you tithe trifles and omit justice. V25 sharpens the spatial dimension: you cleanse the outside and leave the inside full. The two woes complement each other — v23 is about priority inversion (small things vs. great things), v25 is about spatial inversion (outside vs. inside).
- **Matthew 8:2** (cleanse from leprosy) — The LSJ shows kaθaɾιze used in Ev.Matt.8.2 for cleansing from leprosy. Jesus uses the same purification verb in v25 — but there the cleansing is from a disease, here it's only of the exterior surface. The contrast makes the Pharisees' cleansing look like a cure that only treats the skin.
